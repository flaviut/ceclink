does linux automaticalyl figure it out for us?

Not automatically for a standalone USB CEC dongle like the Pulse-Eight architecture.

Linux knows the HDMI connector’s EDID through DRM, and that EDID contains the CEC physical address. But the kernel does not inherently know which DRM HDMI connector your random USB CEC adapter corresponds to. For integrated CEC hardware, DRM/CEC drivers can use the kernel CEC notifier framework to propagate the EDID-derived physical address automatically.

For a Pulse-Eight-style USB dongle, upstream documentation instead recommends something like:

cec-ctl -E /sys/class/drm/card0-HDMI-A-1/edid

That monitors the DRM connector's EDID and updates /dev/cecX's physical address whenever it changes.

So you have three architectures:
1. CEC only on RP2350
   HDMI: pin 13 + GND
   Host must associate /dev/cec0 with the correct DRM connector
   and feed its EDID/physical address to CEC.

2. RP2350 also snoops/reads DDC
   RP2350 learns its own physical address.
   No DRM↔USB association problem.

3. Custom kernel integration
   DRM connector automatically informs your CEC adapter.
   Clean internally, terrible deployment story.

For an off-the-shelf product, #1 is probably still preferable if you can ship a tiny userspace/udev helper.

The awkward part is not extracting the physical address—that's easy. It's figuring out:
/dev/cec0 corresponds to which one?

card0-HDMI-A-1
card0-HDMI-A-2
card1-HDMI-A-1
...

Linux cannot infer that from USB topology because CEC pin 13 has no relationship to USB.

If your physical product is an inline HDMI adapter, though, I'd seriously consider tapping DDC SCL/SDA on the RP2350. Then the hardware knows exactly which HDMI link it's attached to and can extract the physical address from that link's EDID, eliminating the host-side connector-association problem entirely. The physical address sources are explicitly defined by the sink's EDID.

And you don't necessarily need to become an I²C master: if you're inline, you can potentially passively observe the PC's EDID read and parse the relevant bytes. That may be the cleanest product architecture of all.

If you are strictly snooping SCL/SDA and will never drive them, it gets much simpler.

A series diode still doesn’t help much: 5 V minus one diode drop is still around 4.3 V, which is not a safe way to guarantee a 3.3 V GPIO input.

Use either a resistor divider or a 5-V-tolerant input buffer.

For a prototype, a divider on each line is enough:
HDMI DDC SCL ---- 100k ----+---- RP2350 GPIO
                           |
                          150k
                           |
                          GND

At 5 V:
Vgpio = 5 × 150 / (100 + 150)
      = 3.0 V

Same circuit for SDA.

That adds only:
5 V / 250k ≈ 20 µA

of loading to each DDC line, so it barely affects the I²C bus.

For a production design, I would use something like a 74LVC2G17 powered from 3.3 V:
HDMI SCL ──> 5-V-tolerant input ──> RP2350 SCL_GPIO
HDMI SDA ──> 5-V-tolerant input ──> RP2350 SDA_GPIO

Advantages:

    essentially no loading on DDC
    converts cleanly to 3.3 V
    Schmitt-trigger inputs give better noise immunity
    RP2350 is isolated from HDMI-side transients
    still completely passive from the DDC bus's perspective

Then your architecture becomes:
HDMI
 │
 ├── CEC ─────────────── RP2350 CEC TX/RX
 │
 ├── DDC SCL ──buffer──> RP2350 input only
 │
 └── DDC SDA ──buffer──> RP2350 input only

And yes: because you're just sniffing, you do not need an I²C peripheral at all. You can capture SCL/SDA using GPIO/PIO and decode I²C passively. You never ACK, clock-stretch, or pull either line low.

For a bench prototype, yes, a single 100 kΩ series resistor per DDC line will probably work for receive-only sniffing, but it relies on the RP2350's input protection clamp.

Conceptually:
HDMI SCL (≈5 V) ── 100k ── RP2350 GPIO
HDMI SDA (≈5 V) ── 100k ── RP2350 GPIO

When the DDC line is high, the GPIO's protection diode clamps the pin near IOVDD + a diode drop. The 100 kΩ limits injection current to roughly tens of µA:
(5 V - ~3.6 V) / 100k ≈ 14 µA

That's tiny, so as a hack/prototype, I'd expect it to behave.

Two caveats:

    It is technically operating the GPIO beyond its normal input-voltage specification and relying on its protection structure.
    If the XIAO is unpowered while HDMI remains connected, current can leak through that GPIO and partially back-power the RP2350's 3.3 V rail.

A trivially better version costs one more resistor:
HDMI DDC ── 100k ──+── GPIO
                   |
                  150k
                   |
                  GND

Now 5 V becomes ~3 V and you're no longer relying on a clamp. At DDC speeds, 100k/150k should still be fine for sniffing, since the GPIO capacitance is small.

Also, correction to my earlier answer: I was too loose in saying the RP2350 GPIO itself could simply tolerate the 5 V DDC high level. I would not connect 5 V DDC directly to an RP2350 GPIO. The resistor-limited hack is a materially different situation.
